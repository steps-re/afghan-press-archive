The Afghan press, 1871–1930s

Why these volumes matter, and what you are looking at when you open one. This is context for readers arriving cold — not scholarship, and not a substitute for it.

A press arrives

Printing came late to Afghanistan and arrived as lithography rather than movable type. That detail shapes everything you see here. A lithographic page is written out by hand by a skilled calligrapher and then transferred to stone, so these newspapers are, quite literally, manuscripts that happen to be reproduced mechanically. The script is نستعلیق — nasta'liq, the hanging, cursive hand of Persian book culture, which no machine could set and which remains hard for software to read today.

The first Afghan newspaper, شمس النهار Shams al-Nahar, began in Kabul in the 1870s under Amir Sher Ali Khan. It was short-lived. What followed was a long gap.

Siraj al-Akhbar, and a modernist argument

In 1911 Mahmud Tarzi founded سراج الاخبار افغانیه Sirāj al-Akhbār-i Afghāniya, the Torch of the News. Tarzi had spent years in exile in the Ottoman world and returned with a programme: schools, roads, industry, a modern state, and independence from British control of Afghan foreign policy. He argued it fortnightly, in prose, for seven years.

The paper is the reason this archive exists in the form it does. It carried translation and world news alongside polemic, introduced a generation of Afghan writers to the periodical essay, and made a case for national sovereignty that fed directly into the independence won in 1919. Tarzi's daughter Soraya married Amanullah Khan, who became king and pursued much of the programme the paper had spent a decade arguing for.

Its 1,787 surviving pages are here in full, and they are where this project's accuracy was measured — because a modern printed edition of Tarzi's collected articles exists, and could be aligned against the original page images to produce ground truth.

The reform press, and after

Independence in 1919 opened a decade of expansion. امان افغان Amān-i Afghān succeeded Siraj al-Akhbar as the state paper of the Amanullah era, and the 1920s brought provincial papers, journals of education and commerce, and the beginnings of a periodical culture beyond the capital. انیس Anis, founded in 1927 by Muhyi al-Din Anis, outlasted the reform government and ran for decades.

The Amanullah experiment collapsed in 1929. What survived it is partly what you see in this collection: the printed record of an argument about what Afghanistan should become, conducted in public, in Persian, over sixty years.

Why a searchable version matters

These volumes have been digitised as images since NYU's project, and freely available. But an image of a page cannot be searched. A scholar wanting to know where a word first appears, how an idea spread between papers, or what a given issue said about a given week has had to read forward through thousands of pages to find out.

Making the text machine-readable turns 69,624 pages from a set of photographs into something you can ask questions of. The text is imperfect — see method and limits — but it is the difference between a collection you must already know your way around and one you can explore.

Reading these pages fairly

  • The transcription is a finding aid. Use it to locate; read the image to quote.
  • Orthography is period, not modern. Spelling, spacing and letter forms differ from contemporary Persian. Search normalises for this so you do not have to.
  • Dates are dual. Issues typically carry a Hijri and a Gregorian year; Siraj al-Akhbar volume 1 number 1 reads ۱۳۲۹ / 1911.
  • Titles and issue numbers are not yet extracted, so you cannot filter by year. Volume identifiers are NYU's.

Where to start

If you have never opened one of these: begin with Siraj al-Akhbar, volume 1, number 1 — the first issue Tarzi ever printed, masthead and all. Then try searching a word you would expect an argument about modernity to use, such as ترقی (progress) or وطن (homeland), and see where it takes you.
